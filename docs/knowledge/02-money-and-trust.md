# Money, trust and where crypto fits

Sources: Deloitte "The Future of Exchanging Value: Cryptocurrencies and the trust economy";
Chiu & Koeppl (2018); US Treasury (2022).

## What money is for

Money does three jobs: a **unit of account** (prices are quoted in it), a **means of exchange**
(you pay with it) and a **store of value** (it still buys things tomorrow). Deloitte's central
point is that money is a technology for settling obligations between people who don't know or
don't trust each other. Every form of money rests on some kind of trust:

- Government money rests on the state, mainly because taxes must be paid in it.
- Bank money and IOUs rest on trust in the issuer to honour them.
- Commodity money (gold, rum in colonial Australia, cigarettes in prisoner-of-war camps) rests
  on the commodity being scarce and wanted.
- Bitcoin rests on trust that its community of miners and traders will keep it running, liquid
  and accepted. It is not trustless; the trust has moved.

## Why adoption is hard

- **Government attitude decides adoption.** The strongest driver for a currency is a government
  accepting it for taxes; the strongest brakes are bans or taxing it as a commodity.
- **Volatility is a Catch-22.** Big price swings make crypto a poor store of value and force
  merchants to keep repricing. Wider everyday use would calm prices, but the swings block wider
  use. Speculators dominate trading.
- **Complementary currencies** (local currencies, loyalty points, the 1930s Wörgl notes) show
  that alternatives can work in niches and in troubled economies, and fade where the national
  currency works well. Deloitte expected crypto to stay niche in stable economies and matter more
  where local money and banks are weak, and in cross-border transfers.
- **Payments are shifting anyway.** Cards, mobile wallets and online checkout move payment away
  from the till; disruption tends to come from new systems built around customers, not from a
  better coin alone.

## Is Bitcoin good money? (Chiu & Koeppl)

Bitcoin settles payments without a trusted third party, but it pays for that with costly mining
and slow, probabilistic settlement (see [01](01-blockchain-and-bitcoin.md)). In their model the
design at the time was expensive (about 1.4% of consumption); a well-designed cryptocurrency
could be about as cheap as cash with low inflation and could compete with retail payment
systems if scaling were solved.

## What the Treasury found in practice (2022)

- Most people who hold crypto hold it as a **speculative investment**, not for payments. Only
  about 1% of adults used crypto purely for non-investment purposes.
- Promised benefits such as cheaper remittances and banking the unbanked had mostly **not**
  materialised: converting between fiat and crypto, network fees and exchange fees often make it
  dearer than existing options.
- The whole market went from under $200 billion (January 2020) to about $2.9 trillion
  (November 2021) and back below $1 trillion (June 2022). Bitcoin peaked near $1.3 trillion in
  market value in November 2021.

## What this means for the agent

Crypto prices are driven mostly by speculation, sentiment and liquidity, not by cash flows. That
is why the strategies are price-and-volume rules with hard stops, why size is kept small, and why
big macro events (the calendar filter) and regulatory news matter so much.
